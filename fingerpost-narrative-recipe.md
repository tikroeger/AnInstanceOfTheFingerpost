# The Fingerpost Recipe

### How *An Instance of the Fingerpost* is built: four liars, one silent woman, and a false first sentence

> **Spoilers for the whole novel.** References such as **B4.5** mean Book 4, chapter 5 of your epub. The few direct quotations are deliberately short; everything else is paraphrase. Network figures come from the character map built alongside this document (54 people, 3 plot nodes, 159 connections), so they describe that map rather than every name in the book.

---

## 1. The short version

Most novels with several narrators simply hand the camera around. Pears does something stricter. He writes four *documents*, each composed decades after the events by an old man with a case to make, and each written partly as a reply to the others. The reader is not watching the events; the reader is reading the file. By the end you are in the position of Anthony Wood, the fourth narrator: an archivist at a desk covered in contradictory manuscripts. The real question of the book turns out to be not "who killed Dr Grove?" but "how can anyone know anything?"

What makes it work:

- **One compact cluster of events** (Oxford, a few weeks in 1663, one poisoning, one hanging), told four times.
- **Four narrators**, each with a different motive for writing and a different blind spot, matched to Francis Bacon's "idols" of the mind.
- **Lies rationed with discipline:** one outright falsehood, planted in the first sentence; a great many omissions; and, above all, true facts given false meanings.
- **A network that grows outward** book by book (town, then kingdom, then state secrets, then God), so each retelling changes what the story is *about*.
- **Clues scattered across narrators**, so no single narrator can see the pattern but a reader collating them can.
- **A moral centre, Sarah Blundy**, who is the subject of every account and the author of none.
- **Real history used as scaffolding**, with the fiction hidden in genuine gaps in the record.

The rest of this document unpacks each of these and then turns them into a recipe.

## 2. Four documents, one desk

| | Book 1 | Book 2 | Book 3 | Book 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Title** | A Question of Precedence | The Great Trust | The Character of Compliance | An Instance of the Fingerpost |
| **Narrator** | "Marco da Cola", Venetian gentleman-physician | Jack Prestcott, son of a disgraced royalist | Dr John Wallis, mathematician and codebreaker | Anthony Wood, antiquary |
| **Bacon's epigraph** | Idols of the Market: words that take over the understanding | Idols of the Cave: each man's private den | Idols of the Theatre: philosophies that stage invented worlds | The Fingerpost: the decisive instance |
| **Why he writes** | To claim the invention of transfusion and expose false friends | To correct Cola's version and vindicate his father and himself | To explain (not, he insists, to justify) a career of service, and to expose Cola | To sort the three manuscripts Lower sent him, and finally to testify |
| **Who killed Grove?** | Sarah | Thomas Ken | Cola, with poison meant for Wallis | Wood himself, by accident |
| **His Sarah** | Insolent, tempting, guilty | A witch and harlot who bewitched him | A courier for radicals | His lover, then his God |
| **What he hides** | His name, his priesthood, his mission | The rape, the stabbing, the framing; that his "great house" is Bedlam | The judicial murder; his obsession with his servant Matthew; the clue he threw away | His guilt, until he confesses it; Sarah's survival; finally the papers themselves |
| **How he ends** | Home in Venice, philosophy abandoned | Serene and sure of salvation | Afraid that God's mercy will be no greater than his own | Bound by a command to tell no one |

Two things the table makes visible.

**Each narrator trusts a different kind of evidence.** Wood spells this out himself (B4.5). Cola trusts confession: she pleaded guilty, so she did it. Prestcott reasons like a lawyer, asks who gained, and lands on Ken. Wallis trusts the reach of his own logic. Wood then invokes Bacon's fingerpost: the independent, educated eyewitness with nothing to gain, whose testimony settles a question beyond argument. He claims that status for himself, and what he witnessed was himself tipping the powder into Grove's brandy. The one "disinterested" witness is the man with the most at stake. That is the book's joke and its thesis in a single move.

**Each ending is a verdict on the self, and the four endings form a ladder.** Cola withdraws blandly. Jack is monstrously complacent, sure of God's favour from a madhouse cell. Wallis despairs; his last line is "I no longer believe it will" (that God's mercy will prove greater than his). Wood receives grace. The structure is also a moral progression, from a man who thinks he has done nothing wrong to a man who has done wrong and been forgiven.

## 3. Documents in conversation

The narratives are not parallel; they are a correspondence. Richard Lower, the one major figure who never narrates, is the hub. Decades later he receives Cola's memoir and sends it to Prestcott. He sends it to Wallis only when Wallis, by now old and losing his sight, his fame eclipsed by Newton, demands it (B2.1, B3.1). Finally he passes all three manuscripts to Wood with a weary letter: two men can witness the same event and both remember it wrongly, so how can anyone reach certainty? (B4.1)

Three craft consequences follow.

- **Motive and voice come free.** Each narrator writes *against* someone. Prestcott's first page mocks Cola's selective memory and predicts that Wallis will gloat. Wallis's first page reveals that the man he believed drowned is alive. That tells you, on page one of Book 3, how Book 3 ends, and moves the suspense from *what* to *how*.
- **Retelling without repetition.** Because the later narrators have read the earlier ones, they don't re-perform scenes; they cite them and contest the reading. Describing Cola's recoil from Sarah, Wood says it is all in Cola's account and he differs only on interpretation (B4.4). Pears gets four tellings of one Oxford night without making you sit through any of them twice.
- **The reader is promoted to editor.** By Book 4 you have read exactly what Wood has read. When he collates, you collate with him, and you can check his work.

## 4. The network grows outward

The map makes the layering measurable. Here is each person counted once, in the first book where the map records something about them:

| Book | New people | Who arrives | The question the book makes you ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 29 of 54 | Oxford: physicians, dons, the Blundys, the magistrate, the apothecary. Thurloe, the King, Sir James and Ned are only glimpsed or mentioned. | Who poisoned Dr Grove? |
| 2 | 13 | The royalist country-house world: Compton, Mordaunt, Morland, Russell, Collop, Kitty, Clarendon, Bristol, the astrologer Greatorex | Who betrayed the royalist rising? |
| 3 | 9 | The intelligence state: Bennet, the thug Cooth, the merchants and the picture dealer who feed Wallis's files, and the real soldier Marco | Is there a plot against the state? |
| 4 | 3 | Thomas Lower, Aubrey, Tanner, all minor | What was the King doing, and what did they kill? |

Four observations:

- **Book 4 adds almost no one; it adds wiring.** 28 of the map's connections appear only in Book 4: Wood and Grove (killer), Wood and Ned's packet, Cola and the King, Cola and his brother Marco, Bennet and the cipher, Thurloe's men and Anne's broken leg, Locke and Sarah (he noticed she was breathing). The last act is not a reveal of new suspects but a rewiring of familiar faces. That is why it feels inevitable rather than arbitrary.
- **Characters change size.** Sir James Prestcott is never even named in Book 1; Lower dismisses him in a line as a traitor who fled. In Book 2 he is the figure everything revolves around, a wronged hero. In Book 3 he is a would-be regicide, and in Book 4 a secret Catholic who knew the King's secret. Henry Bennet, the Secretary of State who turns out to be the hidden prime mover, is not mentioned at all in Books 1 and 2. Matthew, the centre of Wallis's emotional life, is not named before Book 3, though Cola may have met him in a London alley in Book 1's first chapter. **The most powerful people are introduced last. Power hides.**
- **Few links are seen twice.** Of 159 links, 112 are reported by a single narrator, 37 by two, 10 by three and none by all four. Each narrator owns a slice. The ten links reported three times form the spine of the book: Cola–Lower, Cola–Wallis, Cola–Prestcott, Cola–Matthew, Prestcott–Sarah, Prestcott–Wallis, Prestcott–Thurloe, Sarah–Grove, Grove–Ken and Ned–Sir James.
- **Thirteen people appear in all four books:** the four narrators plus Sarah, Anne, Ned, Grove, Lower, Ken, Sir James, Thurloe and the King. These are the beams; everyone else is infill.

> **Craft lesson:** introduce nearly everybody early and lightly, as furniture, then spend the later books rewiring them. Recognition is more satisfying than introduction: the gossip who scrapes Cola's plate in Book 1 turns out to be the man holding every secret.

## 5. Bacon's idols as a design spec

Pears heads the first three books with Bacon's "idols": in *Novum Organum*, the habitual distortions that stop the mind from seeing nature as it is. They are not decoration. Each one is the operating flaw of the narrator who sits under it.

- **The Market (Book 1, Cola): words.** Bacon's idol of the marketplace is the tyranny of words over understanding. Cola's whole deception is verbal: a name. He says almost nothing false, but he signs himself as someone else, and that one word relabels every true thing he says. His title is also about words: "precedence", meaning who said it first. The priority dispute is itself a decoy (see §6).
- **The Cave (Book 2, Prestcott): the private den.** Each person's temperament and history bend the light. Jack's den is his father. Everything he sees passes through the need for his father to be innocent and himself to be wronged. When that becomes unbearable the filter becomes madness, and demons, witchcraft and horoscopes do the explaining.
- **The Theatre (Book 3, Wallis): systems.** Bacon's idols of the theatre are philosophies that stage fictional worlds. Wallis builds a conspiracy theory so well engineered that no fact can damage it. He even boasts that his hypothesis could absorb corrections "without having to be abandoned" (B3.6), which a modern reader recognises as a theory that cannot be falsified. He is a codebreaker who decodes everything except the people in front of him.
- **The missing idol.** Bacon names four idols: Tribe, Cave, Market and Theatre. Pears uses three. Book 4's epigraph is not the Idol of the Tribe but the fingerpost, the decisive instance. Yet Wood displays exactly what Bacon meant by the Tribe: an error built into human nature, a mind that sees more order than is there and lets its desires steer what it believes. Wood says plainly that what scoffers call coincidence is the visible hand of providence (B4.8), and his love turns Sarah into God. So the fourth idol is present, unnamed, in the narrator who claims to be the fingerpost. Pears lets the reader decide which label fits Book 4.
- **The pun in the title.** Bacon's Latin is *instantia crucis*, named after the signpost at a fork in the road, and *crux* is also a cross. A novel whose final part is a passion story carries a crucifixion inside its scientific title.
- **Instructions in the epigraph.** Book 4's epigraph ends by saying that decisive instances are sometimes found among the evidence already set down. Pears is telling you that the answer is already in Books 1–3, and it is.

## 6. The anatomy of a lie

### 6.1 One lie of commission

Early in Book 4 Wood makes the observation on which the whole design hangs (B4.1). Prestcott may be mad, he says, but Cola is a liar, though perhaps guilty of only **one lie of commission** among all his omissions and evasions. He cites Ammianus: *veritas vel silentio consumitur vel mendacio*, truth is destroyed either by silence or by falsehood. And the single lie sits in a sentence so harmless that even Wallis missed it: the first sentence of the novel.

> "Marco da Cola, Gentleman of Venice, respectfully presents his greetings."

The writer is Andrea da Cola, a Jesuit priest, who has borrowed the name and history of his dead soldier brother. Almost everything that follows is true, but the reader has been given the wrong speaker, so true sentences lead to false conclusions. Wood compares it to scholastic logic: impeccable reasoning from a false premise.

Book 4 shows what that one word was protecting. The memoir exists to prove that *Marco*, a soldier and a layman, was the Cola seen in Whitehall that day. If Marco was in England, the priest Andrea was not, and then no priest received the King into the Catholic Church in 1663 (B4.12). The loud quarrel with Lower over who invented transfusion, the "question of precedence" that gives Book 1 its title and its grievance, is in Wood's reading a smokescreen to draw the eye (B4.12).

Cola even announces his technique in his preface: "I will leave out much, but nothing of significance" (B1.1). It is the most honest-sounding lie in the book.

**Why this works so well.** A lie about a fact can be checked. A lie about the frame cannot, because every check is made from inside the frame. And because this is a lie about identity, it changes the meaning of everything retroactively: Cola's recoil from Sarah's offer (a priest's horror at himself, not a gentleman's anger, B4.4), his secret anointing of her dying mother, his fluency in theology, his interest in an obscure heresy, his silence about soldiering.

Notice the gradient. Cola lies through the frame and through omission. Prestcott hardly omits anything: he narrates his crimes in detail and gets their meaning wrong. Wallis includes the evidence against himself and refuses to look at it. Wood confesses everything about himself and then chooses silence about the most important thing he knows. These are four different relationships between a narrator and the truth, not four flavours of one "unreliable narrator".

Wood's opening claim to be disinterested deserves a second look. Disinterest is the formal qualification Bacon's fingerpost requires, and Wood claims it twice (B4.1, B4.5). It is the biggest lie in Book 4, and he tells it to himself before he tells it to us.

### 6.3 A society that runs on lies

The narrators are not the only liars, and the background lying is what makes the foreground believable:

- **Ken** tells the Warden he saw Sarah slip into Grove's room. He cannot take it back without admitting that he came upon Jack escaping and said nothing (B2.14, B4.8).
- **Jack** pays a market girl to say that Grove groped her.
- **Morland** forged letters for Thurloe, and **Mordaunt** betrayed the 1659 rising on the King's own orders. The "traitor" Sir James was framed to hide a worse truth: he had tried to kill the King (B3.11).
- **Thurloe** stages a mock trial to talk Jack out of killing him.
- **Bennet** tells Wallis to leave the Venetians' London agent alone; it is none of his concern.

This is the Restoration: a political class that had changed sides and rewritten its past. Wallis's defence of his own career, that he served whatever power was in being for the sake of order (B3.1), supplies his book's title, *The Character of Compliance*, and describes the period too.

The most important lie of all is merciful. Lower, Locke and Wood fake Sarah's dissection using another woman's corpse (B4.11). Pears refuses to make lying the villain; the question is always what a lie serves.

### 6.4 True facts, false meanings

This is Pears's favourite move, and it is why the reader never feels cheated. He keeps the facts fixed and rotates their meaning. Nothing in Book 1 is retracted; it is re-explained.

- **The arsenic.** Book 1: the apothecary's ledger shows that Sarah bought a large quantity four months before Grove died (B1.14). True. Book 4: Grove himself explains that he laid in a big supply of the powder around that time as a purgative, on the advice of the King's physician and against Cola's warning that it was dangerous (B4.5). She bought it on her master's errand.
- **Sarah's walks to Abingdon.** Wood, jealous, assumes she is selling herself to soldiers. Wallis, paranoid, assumes she is carrying messages to radicals. Wood follows her and finds that she preaches in a trance at secret meetings (B4.3, B4.6). One fact, three idols. Wood names the principle himself: start from faulty assumptions and you can build a false conclusion out of facts that are all true (B4.6).
- **Grove's ring**, found on Sarah, was planted by Jack (B1.15, then B2.14).
- **Romans 8:13**, quoted by Ken at the college dinner (B1.8), becomes in Jack's hands both a motive and a confession (B2.1).
- **The barred door.** After the hanging the apothecary physically blocks Cola from Lower's workroom (B1.18). Cola reads it as the final insult in a soured friendship. Upstairs, Sarah is breathing.
- **The man at the play.** At a performance of *Lear*, Cola watches a thin, humble man approach Wallis and be snubbed, and reads his face: "one of nature's supplicants", born to obey. Wood tells him it is John Thurloe, Cromwell's spymaster (B1.14). The novel shows its whole method in miniature here: a clever man reads surfaces with confidence and gets them comically wrong.
- **Anne's leg.** The injury that sets Book 1 going, and the reason Cola meets the Blundys at all, looks like an accident. In Book 4 Anne says she was pushed by a man from Thurloe's office who was searching for Ned's papers (B4.4). The whole plot begins with a state search.

## 7. One death, every hand on it

Grove's death is a chain in which all four narrators are links, and the only person punished is the one who is not.

1. **Jack** spreads a story that Grove and Sarah were lovers, through Wood the gossip, and pays a market girl to back it up (B2).
2. **Grove**, told by Jack that Wood placed Sarah in his household to discredit him, turns on Wood and threatens to shame him and his mother (B4.5).
3. **Wood**, humiliated, tips the packet of purgative from Grove's table into his brandy. He meant it as a spiteful prank to give Grove a bad night (B4.5).
4. That night **Jack**, escaping jail in Wallis's cloak and hat, finds Grove dying and takes his key and signet ring. He later uses the ring to frame Sarah (B2.14, B1.15).
5. **Ken**, who came upon Jack during the escape, says nothing and sticks to his lie about Sarah (B4.8).
6. **Cola** carries Jack's story about the ring to the magistrate (B1).
7. **Wallis** believes the poison was Cola's and meant for himself (B3.1). He still lets Sarah hang in exchange for Jack's evidence, steered there by Thurloe, who wants Ned Blundy's papers to stay buried (B3.11).
8. **Sarah's alibi** is that she was in Wood's bed. She has made him swear not to use it, and at her trial she pleads guilty (B1.17, B4.10).

Every narrator has a reason to misremember this one night. That is what makes four tellings of it worth having.

Two principles sit underneath the table.

**Plant the decisive clue in several wrong mouths.** The proof that "Marco" is Andrea needs Jack's description (Book 2), Bushrod's description and Jackson's letter (Book 3), and Wood's search of a trunk (Book 4). None of the first three narrators can see the pattern, because each holds only one piece and each has an idol that explains it away. Only a collator can solve it.

**Disguise plants as digression or characterisation.** Prisca looks like Cola showing off, the *Lear* scene looks like comic face-reading, and the brawl looks like scene-setting. Readers skim what looks like texture, and that is exactly where Pears hides the load-bearing pieces.

## 9. Sarah: the subject of every story, the author of none

Sarah never narrates. Everything we know of her comes through four men who each have reasons to see her a particular way. The text makes the imbalance measurable. Her name appears about 65 times in Cola's account and 22 in Jack's. In Wallis's 58,000 words it appears about a dozen times, from the man who let her hang. In Wood's it appears more than 160 times. Wallis can barely bring himself to name her; at the end she haunts him as "that girl".

Each idol produces a different Sarah:

- **Cola (words, appearances):** a proud, insolent servant who offers herself as payment for her mother's treatment, and must therefore be capable of murder.
- **Jack (the den):** a witch and harlot who cursed him. On his very first page he also states that she did not kill Grove, without noticing the contradiction (B2.1).
- **Wallis (the system):** a node in a radical network.
- **Wood (the tribe: love and providence):** his lover, and then the Messiah.

Underneath, Pears lays out a complete Passion in fragments through Book 4:

- **An annunciation:** Anne's dream of a dove naming the child (B4.10).
- **A magus:** Greatorex saw her in his charts and came over the sea to kneel, and Wood can't stop himself quoting the Gospel (B4.10).
- **Gethsemane:** Wood's careless joke that, unlike most prophets, she hopes the cup will pass from her. She catches the allusion; he doesn't, until later (B4.6).
- **A betrayer:** Jack, born under Judas's stars.
- **A cock crowing in the night** as Wood fails her (B4.11).
- **Death and rising** on the dissecting table.
- **A messianic secret:** "tell no one".
- **An ascension:** she vanishes from a becalmed ship in broad daylight (B4.11).

The doctrine that makes all of this cohere had already been read to us, as a throwaway, by Cola in Book 1: Prisca's Messiah reborn in every generation.

Pears never settles it. Lower thinks she was simply never dead (B4.11). Wood says that anyone who wishes to disbelieve him may do so (B4.12). The book hands you the fingerpost and does not say which way it points.

Her silence is also partly her own choice. She forbids Wood to give her alibi, she pleads guilty, and she commands him to keep the secret. Her silence is a choice, not just something done to her.

> **Craft lesson:** put the moral weight on the one person denied a voice. Her silence is not an absence. It is the empty space every narrator projects into, and the reader feels that injustice more than any argument could make them feel it.

## 10. History as load-bearing structure

Most of the cast is real: Boyle, Locke, Lower, Wren, Wallis, Wood, Thurloe, Bennet, Clarendon, Bristol, Morland, Mordaunt, Ken, Grove, Stahl, Crosse, Warden Woodward, and Greatorex (the healer Valentine Greatrakes). The cast notes at the end say that Sarah and the Prestcotts are invented, but each is modelled on a real case. Sarah is based on Anne Greene, hanged in Oxford, who famously revived on the anatomists' table. The Prestcotts are based on Sir Richard Willys, disgraced for treason in 1660, whose son died insane.

More striking are the specific gaps in the record that Pears fills:

- **A one-line death.** The real Wood's diary records the death of Robert Grove, a senior fellow of New College, on 30 March 1663, and says nothing more (cast notes). Pears turns that line into a murder whose secret author is the diarist himself.
- **A soured temperament.** The cast notes say the real Wood had a wide circle of friends until the 1660s and later became a reclusive, rancorous man. In Book 4 Wood says he acquired his reputation for oddity and ill temper in exactly those days (B4.12). The novel supplies the broken heart.
- **Burned papers.** The book closes with a real note by Thomas Hearne: Wood set papers aside, and his executor Tanner burned them when Wood gave the sign. Book 4 thereby presents itself as one of the documents that history records as destroyed.
- **A secret conversion.** Charles II's 1663 approach to Rome is real; it was not published until 1903 (cast notes). The novel's biggest secret is a historical one.
- **Hidden archives.** Thurloe really did hide his state papers in a ceiling (cast notes). Ned's packet under Wood's floorboards rhymes with it.

> **Craft lesson:** the archive gives you constraints (who was where), plausibility (real names carry weight) and, above all, *gaps*. Pears never contradicts the record. He lives inside its silences and makes those silences his subject.

## 11. Voice as method

Each voice is a way of knowing as well as a style, and each one's strength is also its blind spot.

- **Cola, the experimentalist.** Observant, curious and vain, faintly comic about English food and manners. He looks hard at symptoms and faces and draws confident conclusions. His learning is his tell: Wood later points out that the soldier Marco had no reputation for study, while the man in Oxford could discuss ancient and modern authors with ease.
- **Jack, the gentleman.** Honour, land and lineage. He reasons with his tutor's three-part logic (B2.10), logic in the service of a fixed conclusion, and when logic fails he turns to astrology and demons. His bluff confidence is his camouflage: you stop trusting him exactly when he is most persuasive.
- **Wallis, the cryptographer.** His narrative is a dossier: dated, cross-referenced, with letters appended so that no one can call him careless. He hears patterns everywhere, and a dinner-table joke about highwaymen becomes evidence of an assassination plot. His rigour makes his errors worse, because a system defends them.
- **Wood, the antiquary.** Digressive and self-deprecating, he cites authorities on jealousy and witchcraft. He believes in documents and in God, and in the end trusts his own eyes over his methods.

Under all four runs the book's intellectual argument. The new experimental science (Boyle's air pump, Stahl testing the brandy on cats, Lower's transfusions) can repeat an event until it is certain, but human events happen only once. Wood says so in his first chapter: he cannot rerun that night the way Stahl reran his chemicals (B4.1). History and justice rest on testimony, and testimony passes through idols. That is how a 1663 murder mystery becomes a book about the limits of knowledge without ever turning into a lecture.

## 12. The recipe

### Ingredients

1. **One compact cluster of events:** a few weeks, one town, a death at the centre.
2. **Four narrators with conflicting interests**, each with a specific, characterful distortion.
3. **One innocent at the centre who never narrates.**
4. **A real historical gap** to hide in.
5. **A thematic key** (here, Bacon) that tells the reader *how* to read without telling them *what* to conclude.

### Method

**1. Write documents, not viewpoints.** Give each narrator a reason to write, a time of writing and a reader to persuade. In *Fingerpost* each account replies to another, and motive, voice and selectivity follow automatically.

**2. Give each narrator a governing idol.** Not "unreliable" in general, but wrong in one consistent way that grows out of character: vanity about words, obsession with family, love of systems, love and faith. Readers learn to correct for each lens, and that correcting is the pleasure.

**3. Ration the lies.** Use one outright falsehood, placed where nobody checks (a signature, a name, a date in the first paragraph), and many omissions. Most lies in good fiction are silences.

**4. Keep facts fixed; rotate meanings.** Let later narrators re-explain earlier scenes rather than contradict them. The arsenic was bought, the ring was found, she did walk to Abingdon. The reader never feels cheated, because nothing they were told was false; only what they inferred was.

**5. Make every narrator complicit in the central event.** If each holds a link in the chain, each has a reason to bend the same night. Four tellings of one night only work if four people have something to hide about it.

**6. Widen the frame each time.** Town, kingdom, state, God. Each book should change not just *who did it* but *what kind of story this is*: murder mystery, revenge tragedy, spy novel, passion.

**7. Introduce the whole cast early, then rewire it.** Book 4 adds three minor people and 28 new connections. Revelations should be new links between old characters.

**8. Hide the powerful longest.** Bennet doesn't exist until Book 3. Thurloe first appears as a humble face at a play. In this book, power is measured by how late you learn about it.

**9. Spread the decisive clue across narrators.** Make sure no narrator holds two pieces of it. Only a reader who collates can solve it, so make the reader a collator.

**10. Hide plants inside digression and characterisation.** A showy theological aside, a comic scene of face-reading, a one-line brawl. Readers skim what looks like texture.

**11. Let each narrator announce his method and lie in the same breath.** "Nothing of significance." "Disinterested." "Not to justify, but to explain." Each preface is a fingerpost pointing the wrong way.

**12. Make the last narrator the one you overlooked.** In Book 1 Wood is a shabby gossip eating Cola's leftovers, and the only man who stands up for Sarah at her trial, to jeers. All along he held the packet, the codebook and the stolen letters, and it was his hand that spiked Grove's brandy.

**13. Put the moral weight on the voiceless.** The person everyone uses as evidence should be the one person denied a document. Let her silence be partly her own choice.

**14. Anchor in the real record and live in its gaps.** A one-line diary entry, a burned bundle of papers, a secret conversion, a soured temperament. Fill the gaps; never contradict the record.

**15. Answer every answerable question, then leave one fingerpost pointing two ways.** Resolve the puzzle completely: who killed Grove, who Cola was, what the cipher said. Leave the mystery open: was Sarah dead, and was she divine? Readers forgive an unanswered question only once every answerable one has been answered.

**16. End each narrative on a self-judgment.** Each ending shows how a narrator has judged himself, and together they form a moral ladder.

## 13. How this goes wrong, and how Pears avoids it

- **Repetition fatigue.** Replaying scenes in full four times is deadly. Pears's narrators have read each other, so they summarise and argue, and new information drives every retelling.
- **The final narrator as God.** If Book 4 simply told the truth, Books 1–3 would become puzzles to throw away. Wood is right about Grove and about Cola, admits he is guessing about Matthew, and may be deluded about Sarah. The truth-teller has his own idol.
- **Cheating.** A twist that depends on a falsehood the reader could never have detected feels like a trick. Pears allows himself one lie of commission and hangs the solution on clues he has already given.
- **Puzzle without heart.** The machinery would be cold if the dead and the hanged didn't matter. Sarah's suffering, Anne's death, Wallis's grief for Matthew and Wood's love give every gear moral weight.
- **History as costume.** Here the period detail *is* the plot: transfusion, the Restoration settlement, ciphers, the persecution of dissenters, the fear of popery.

## 14. A reading exercise with the map

Open the character map and turn off Books 2–4. Book 1 alone is an Oxford murder, solved. Turn on Book 2 and the royalist world floods in, and the solution flips. Book 3 adds the state. Then turn on Book 4 and watch how few new boxes appear and how many new lines. Click any of Sarah's entries to see how each man described her.
